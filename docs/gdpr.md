# GDPR for people who run Swarm

This guide is for a publisher or install owner in the EU (or with EU readers) who runs their own Swarm. It explains who is responsible for what, what personal data Swarm keeps and why, and which features help you meet the GDPR. It is practical help, not legal advice: your own situation decides what you must do, and a lawyer or your data protection authority has the last word.

Words used here: a **subscriber** is someone on a publication's list; a **teammate** is someone who signs in to the dashboard; the **install owner** is whoever runs this copy of Swarm and its cloud accounts.

## Who is responsible

Swarm is software you run yourself. There is no Swarm company in the middle: no hosted service receives your data, and the people who write Swarm never see it. The data sits in your own Cloudflare account and goes only to the services you connect.

| Role                                  | Who                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Controller**                        | Whoever decides why and how subscribers' data is used. On an install that only you use, that is you. On an install with several workspaces, each workspace's owner is normally the controller for their own subscribers, and the install owner is also the controller for teammates' accounts and for how the install itself is run.                                         |
| **Processor for other workspaces**    | If you run Swarm for other people (clients, a network of publications, anyone who is not you), you process their subscribers' data on their behalf. You then need a data processing agreement (Art. 28) with each of them, and the services below become your subprocessors.                                                                                                 |
| **Processors (services you connect)** | Cloudflare (hosting, database, file storage, bot checks, and Workers AI for Copilot by default); one email service (Amazon SES, Resend, Mailgun or Postmark, or your own mail server); Stripe and, if a publication connects it, PayPal for payments; Anthropic only if you choose it for Copilot. [Subprocessors](subprocessors.md) lists what each one receives and where. |
| **Not processors of yours**           | Mailbox providers (Gmail and others) receive email addressed to their users. Analytics or ad tools a publication adds to its website are chosen by that publication's owner: they are that owner's processors, or separate controllers, and that owner is responsible for them.                                                                                              |

## Lawful bases newsletters usually rely on

Swarm does not choose these for you. These are the usual choices; check them against your own case.

- **Consent** (Art. 6(1)(a)) for sending the newsletter, and the ePrivacy rules on electronic marketing for most marketing email. Use confirm opt-in and a consent checkbox on your forms (see below).
- **Contract** (Art. 6(1)(b)) for paid subscriptions: delivering what someone paid for, receipts and account emails.
- **Legal obligation** (Art. 6(1)(c)) for keeping invoices and payment records as tax law requires (Stripe and PayPal keep their own).
- **Legitimate interest** (Art. 6(1)(f)) is sometimes used for measuring opens and clicks, for security logs and for fraud checks on referrals. If you rely on it, write down your balancing test, tell readers in your privacy notice, and let them object. Several EU authorities expect consent for per-person open and click tracking in email, so a new publication starts with it off; turn it on only once you have that consent (see [privacy controls](privacy-controls.md)).

Website code that sets cookies or tracks visitors (analytics, ad pixels) needs the visitor's consent under the ePrivacy rules unless it is strictly necessary. Swarm's own website analytics use no cookies.

**Copilot keeps no text.** What a teammate types to Copilot (the instruction, the selected text) and what the model answers are not stored by Swarm. Each request leaves a metadata row (`copilot_requests`: who, which publication and feature, model, token counts, status and time), and every call Copilot makes through the API is in the agent log with that request's id (`copilot_request_id`) and its masked arguments, so an action can be traced to the request without keeping what was asked. This is on purpose (data minimisation): don't expect to find past instructions in Swarm. What the AI provider keeps is set by its own terms.

## Privacy notice checklist

Your privacy notice (Art. 13 and 14) should cover at least:

- [ ] Who you are and how to contact you (and your data protection officer, if you have one).
- [ ] What you collect: address, name, any profile fields you ask for, how they joined, opens and clicks if you track them, payments if you sell.
- [ ] Why, and the lawful basis for each purpose (see above).
- [ ] Who receives it: your hosting (Cloudflare), your email service, your payment services, and any website tools you add. You can link to your copy of [subprocessors.md](subprocessors.md).
- [ ] Transfers outside the EEA and the safeguard for each (usually the EU-US Data Privacy Framework or standard contractual clauses).
- [ ] How long you keep each kind of data.
- [ ] Their rights: access, correction, erasure, restriction, portability, objection, withdrawing consent at any time, and complaining to a data protection authority.
- [ ] That every email has an unsubscribe link, and where readers manage their own settings.
- [ ] Your cookie policy for the website, if you add any tools that set cookies. Link it from the cookie popup (**Website → Cookies and code**).
- [ ] For a shared install: who the controller is for each publication.

## Handling requests from subscribers

Readers can do most things themselves, and the rest goes through the **Privacy Center** (**Settings → Privacy Center**).

- **Access and portability.** A reader asks from their **Subscriber settings** page (linked in every email), or you start a request for them under **Privacy Center → New request → A copy of their data**. Swarm builds a file of what it holds on that address in the workspace (each publication's subscriber record and history, and the rows that hold the address without a subscription) and emails a download link that works for 7 days. The file lists what it leaves out on purpose and why (for example the audit log and teammates' saved views). The request is due within 24 hours.
- **Erasure.** A reader asks from the same page, or you choose **Their data to be deleted**. Swarm deletes the address in every publication of the workspace, with its history, and keeps only a hash so the address is never added again. A teammate's request needs an admin to approve it. A reader's request is confirmed by a single-use link emailed to the address; when Swarm can't email that address (it bounced or complained), the request always waits for an admin to approve it, whichever link the reader opened the page from, so confirm with the person another way first. It is due within 30 days. Erasure waits while a Stripe subscription is still renewing, because it could not be cancelled afterwards. **Delete subscriber** on one publication is not a full erasure: it leaves the address in blocked addresses, orders, checkouts, Gift Passes, trial claims, email checks and bounce history. Use the Privacy Center for a request, before or after deleting. A request needs only the address, not a subscriber, so it also covers a buyer, gift recipient or sponsor contact who never subscribed; Swarm creates no subscriber for it and answers the same whether or not it holds anything.
- **People who are not subscribers can ask themselves.** Each publication has a public request page, `<capture address>/privacy/<publication id>`, linked as **Your data** in the website footer and from subscriber settings; link it from your privacy notice too. Anyone enters their address and chooses a copy or deletion. The page answers the same whatever is held, and only emails the address a single-use link (24 hours); the request is filed when that link's page is used, so only the inbox can ask, and it then runs like a confirmed request from subscriber settings (source **Request page, confirmed by email** in the Privacy Center). It sends one email per address and kind every 10 minutes at most, accepts 5 requests an hour from one network address and 3 a day for one email address, and emails nothing to an address that bounced, complained or was erased: those people contact you, and you file the request in the Privacy Center. A deletion waits while a Stripe subscription still renews.
- **Correction.** Readers change their name on their settings page; you can edit anything else on the subscriber.
- **Objection and withdrawing consent.** One click on unsubscribe, or the mail app's own unsubscribe button. A reader can also pause emails for a while.
- **Requests that arrive another way** (an email to you, a letter): check who is asking, then start the request in the Privacy Center so it is tracked and logged.

When a request is rejected, Swarm emails the address that it was declined and that the person can reply to ask why; the email never says why or what is held, and never includes your note. When a request fails for good, the person is told it is delayed, and the workspace's owners and admins are emailed every 3 days until someone retries or rejects it. A request whose sealed address can't be opened (the key it was sealed with is no longer in `FIELD_KEYS`) fails at once with a note naming that key: put the key back and retry, or reject it and file it again. After an erasure, every request about that address keeps only "The address was erased." in place of notes and errors.

Every step is written to the workspace's audit log, without the address. The GDPR gives you one month to answer most requests; the Privacy Center shows what is due and what is overdue.

## What Swarm gives you

- **Confirm opt-in.** New subscribers confirm by email before they receive anything, and you can make every subscriber confirm. The confirm link only opens a page; the button confirms, so mail scanners cannot confirm people. See [Audience](audience.md#confirm-opt-in).
- **Consent records.** For each subscriber, the date they agreed, the date they confirmed, and the wording and form version of the form. When the sign-up named an older version of the form than the one Swarm serves now (a cached page, an embed copied earlier, or a hand-made request), the record keeps that version's wording. Every form Swarm renders (the embed frame, and the forms on the publication's own website) carries a stamp the server signed with the form, its version and the time it was rendered; when the sign-up brings a valid stamp, the record says the version was shown by Swarm (`stamped_at`, the time of rendering) even if a cached page showed an older one. Without a stamp (an embed copied as HTML, a hand-made request, or a page cached for more than 90 days) the version is marked as claimed (`version_claimed`), because Swarm can't show it was presented. Either way that version's required box is enforced.
- **One-click unsubscribe.** Every email carries a working unsubscribe link and the standard one-click header that Gmail, Apple Mail and others use. Unsubscribing never needs a sign-in.
- **Shortened network addresses** everywhere Swarm keeps one, and no full addresses.
- **Cookieless website analytics.** Swarm's own website sets no tracking cookie and loads nothing from other companies unless the publication adds a tool. The cookies it can set are functional: a reader's sign-in session, the cookie popup's choice, and, on a publication with free reads, a meter cookie that lists the posts read this month or week on that site (no identifier, kept in the browser only, gone after the period). Tools added under **Website → Cookies and code** wait for each reader's agreement in the cookie popup. If an owner marks a known tracking tool as strictly necessary, Swarm asks them to confirm and records it.
- **Encrypted sensitive fields and keys**, and only hashes of sign-in links and session cookies.
- **Tracking and retention settings.** See [privacy controls](privacy-controls.md).

## Email checks with DeBounce

When the person running Swarm sets up [email checks](email-verification.md), Swarm sends subscribers' addresses to DeBounce, which then processes them for you:

- **What goes to DeBounce**: the email address, nothing else. Signups through a form that asks people to confirm are sent only after the person confirmed; on a single opt-in form, only if you turn on checking there, at signup. Signups and additions are sent one at a time; list checks send parts of up to 5,000 addresses, which DeBounce fetches from your own Swarm at a random address that stops working when DeBounce has finished, or after 24 hours.
- **What Swarm keeps**: one result per address per workspace (its colour, DeBounce's code and words, any suggested spelling, who checked and when). The result of a signup that was refused is not kept.
- **Erasure**: erasing an address in the Privacy Center deletes its result. A part already sent to DeBounce is held by DeBounce under its own terms: ask DeBounce to delete it if you need to.
- **Your notice**: name DeBounce as a recipient and say why (to keep bounces low and catch typing mistakes). Accept DeBounce's data processing terms before you turn it on.

## Where data is stored and international transfers

- **Database and files.** Your D1 database and R2 buckets are created once, in one place, when you install. `pnpm swarm` creates them without a location hint, so Cloudflare places them near where the install ran from. If you need them in the EU, check the location in the Cloudflare dashboard, or set up by hand with `node scripts/provision.mjs production --location weur` (or `eeur`) as described in [deployment](deployment.md). A location hint says where the data is placed; read Cloudflare's own documentation if you need a legal guarantee that it stays in the EU. More in [Data residency](data-residency.md).
- **Requests** are served from Cloudflare's network close to each visitor, wherever they are.
- **Email** goes through the region you chose for your email service (for example an EU region of Amazon SES, or Mailgun's EU region).
- **Payments** go through Stripe and PayPal under their own transfer terms.
- **Copilot** uses Cloudflare Workers AI by default; Anthropic, if you choose it, is in the United States.
- **Email checks**, if you set them up, send addresses to DeBounce: check where it processes them in its terms.

Most of these companies are certified under the EU-US Data Privacy Framework or offer standard contractual clauses. Check each one, and record which applies.

## Security incidents and breaches

If personal data is lost, changed or seen by someone who should not see it, you may have to tell your data protection authority within 72 hours (Art. 33) and, when the risk is high, the people affected (Art. 34). Swarm helps you find out what happened:

- **The audit log** of each workspace (**Settings → Audit log**) records who did what and when, with a shortened network address. It is hash-chained, so editing or removing an entry is detected, and a daily check flags a broken chain. You can export it as CSV or JSONL. It is kept for **Settings > Privacy > Keep the audit log for** (2 years by default), or less where the workspace's plan keeps it for a shorter time, and what is left still verifies after old entries are removed. An owner can remove a former teammate's details from the workspace (**Settings > Privacy > Former teammates**); see [privacy controls](privacy-controls.md#how-long-other-records-are-kept).
- **Settings → Devices** shows where each teammate is signed in, and signs a device out.
- **Cloudflare's own logs and account audit log** cover access to the cloud account itself, which Swarm cannot see.

Swarm does not detect intrusions, alert you to unusual exports or write the notification for you. Keep your Cloudflare and email service accounts protected with a second factor, and keep the recovery kit safe.

## Agreements to sign

Sign or accept a data processing agreement with each service you use. Most are part of the service's terms or can be accepted in its dashboard:

- **Cloudflare**: its data processing addendum (self-serve, in the Cloudflare dashboard).
- **Your email service**: the AWS GDPR data processing addendum (part of the AWS service terms), or the DPA of Resend, Mailgun or Postmark.
- **Stripe** and **PayPal**: their data processing terms, for each publication's own account.
- **Anthropic**: its commercial terms and DPA, only if you use it for Copilot.
- **DeBounce**: its data processing terms, only if you set up email checks.
- **Google**: only if teammates sign in with Google.
- **Each workspace you run for someone else**: a DPA between you and them, listing your subprocessors.

## What Swarm does not do for you

- It does not decide your lawful basis or write your privacy notice or cookie policy.
- It does not sign agreements with services for you, or check that you have them.
- It does not keep data in the EU by itself: where the database lives depends on how you set it up, and requests are served worldwide.
- It does not manage consent for website tools beyond its own popup, and it cannot tell whether code a publication adds is really necessary; it only asks about tools it recognises.
- It does not delete data from the services it sends to: email service logs, Stripe and PayPal records, exports teammates downloaded, your backups, or copies in your own export destination and webhook receivers. Handle those under each service's own controls. What it does: an export destination gets a `deletions/` line whose `memberId` matches the `id` of that person's earlier `members/` lines, and a member line with `trackingOptOutAt` when someone objects to tracking, so the receiving system can drop their earlier events; webhooks get `member.deleted`. A finished erasure lists, on its Privacy Center request, the export destinations and webhook hosts that may still hold earlier copies, so you know where to follow up. A deleted publication's export destinations stop running while it can be restored.
- After an erasure, two payment references stay on rows kept for your books, detached from the person: the payment id (Stripe or PayPal) on payments and the Stripe checkout session id on checkouts (`RETAINED_PROVIDER_REFERENCES` in `privacy.ts`), so refunds, disputes and reconciliation can still find the payment at the provider. The basis is your legal obligation to keep accounting records (Art. 6(1)(c), and Art. 17(3)(b) for not erasing them at once); they are used for nothing else. **Settings > Privacy > Keep payment references after a deletion request** (6, 7, 8 or 10 years, default 10, counted from each payment; API `paymentRecordYears` on `/api/v1/workspaces/{workspaceId}/retention`) sets how long: after that the jobs worker removes them (a payment's id becomes `expired:<row id>`, a checkout's session id is cleared) and keeps the amounts, currencies and dates. Tax record periods differ by country: choose your country's. The same applies to payments of a deleted publication once it is purged. Anyone holding both the database and the Stripe or PayPal account can link a kept reference back to the customer there until it is removed, and the provider keeps its own records under its own terms.
- An address that hard-bounced on an install that sends with Amazon SES may stay on the SES account-level suppression list, in clear, after an erasure, and that list blocks mail to it from every workspace of the install. Swarm keeps it there on purpose and does not remove it: staying suppressed is what keeps the erased person from being mailed again, which is what they asked for, and Swarm itself keeps only a one-way marker. The finished erasure request says so. Only hard bounces are put on that list (the mailbox doesn't exist), never complaints or erasures as such; a queued change for an address no other workspace holds is dropped by the erasure. Resend, Mailgun and Postmark keep their own bounce and complaint lists in your account with them, which Swarm doesn't write to or remove from. Record this in your records of processing: the basis is that the entry serves the person's own request not to be contacted.
- Restoring the database from a backup or point-in-time copy brings back people erased since that point, and the restored database has no record of those erasures. Swarm has no replay for them. Before restoring, decide how you will repeat the erasures made after the restore point (for example from the people's original requests, kept outside Swarm).
- It does not answer requests that arrive by email or letter by itself: start them in the Privacy Center.
- It does not notify authorities or people affected by a breach.
- It does not carry out a data protection impact assessment for you, if your use needs one.
