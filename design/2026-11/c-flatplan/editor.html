    Insert

    Open a small site on your phone this week and count. A cookie banner slides up from the bottom. A newsletter box fades in over the first paragraph. Somewhere around the second scroll, a chat bubble offers help nobody asked for. On many independent sites a reader now meets three popups before reading a single sentence, and a good share of them leave before the fourth.

    What a popup costs

    The case for the popup is always a number: signups went up eleven percent the week it went live. What the number leaves out is everyone who closed the tab. They never show up in a report, because a reader who leaves in four seconds looks exactly like one who was never there. Readers learn to dismiss whatever interrupts them, and our own logs from last spring showed the close button getting faster every week.

    There is a quieter cost too. A popup tells the reader what the site wants before the site has given anything. It turns the first moment of a visit into a negotiation. Writers who would never open a conversation by asking for a phone number do the equivalent on every page load, then wonder why return visits stay flat. The reader survey we ran in September said it plainly: people subscribe to writing they have already enjoyed.

    Ask once, at the end, when the reader has had something worth staying for.

    Paywall break

    Paid subscribers read on below this line. Free subscribers see a prompt to upgrade.

    For paid subscribers, here are the numbers from our own test. We removed every popup from Field Notes for six weeks and moved a single signup form to the end of each post. Signups dipped for nine days, then recovered, and the people who joined opened more of what we sent.

    A better ask

    • Put the form after the last paragraph, not before the first.
    • Say what arrives and how often: one email, Tuesday mornings.
    • Never ask twice in the same visit.
