Notes on the popup economy

No preview text yet, so this shows the post's first line.

Insert

On many small sites a reader now meets three popups before reading one sentence. The first asks about cookies, the second offers ten percent off, and the third slides up from the corner wanting an email address for a newsletter the reader has not yet had a reason to want. By the time the page is clear, the thing they came for has gone a little cold.

What a popup costs

Every interruption spends something the writer never sees on a dashboard. A signup rate is easy to count; the reader who closed the tab is not. A form that appears at second three asks for trust the page has not earned yet, and most people answer the only way they can, by leaving.

The pattern shows up when publishers change it. One comparison of independent newsletters found that moving the prompt from a timed overlay to the end of the post lowered raw signups slightly but raised the share of new subscribers who opened their first three issues. Fewer people joined, and more of them stayed.

Ask once, at the end, when the reader has had something worth staying for.

Paywall break
Paid subscribers read on below this line. Free subscribers see a prompt to upgrade.

For paid subscribers, the longer version: the three changes we made to Field Notes this spring, and what each one did to signups, opens and cancellations over twelve weeks. None of them is clever. All of them are a little slower than a popup.

A better ask

  • One prompt, placed after the last paragraph, never on top of it.
  • Say what arrives and how often: one essay, every Friday.
  • Let people read the archive first, without handing over an address.
