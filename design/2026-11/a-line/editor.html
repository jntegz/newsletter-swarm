FN Field Notes06:00 Notes on the popup economy

Insert

On a lot of small sites now, a reader meets three popups before reading a single sentence. The first asks about cookies, the second offers ten percent off something they have not seen yet, and the third, usually the newsletter box, slides up from the corner just as they find the opening line. By the time the page is clear, the reason they came has gone cold. Most of them close the tab, and the writer never learns why.

What a popup costs

Every interruption spends a small amount of the reader’s attention, and attention is the only thing a small publication really has to trade. The cost does not show up in a dashboard. A signup form reports its conversions and nothing else, so it always looks as if it is working. What it cannot report is the reader who left before the second paragraph, or the one who stayed but read with one eye on the close button. In last spring’s reader survey, the most common complaint was not the price or how often the issues arrive. It was the boxes.

None of this means a publication should stop asking. It means the ask should come at a moment that makes sense to the reader. A prompt at the top of a post asks for trust before anything has been offered. The same prompt at the end of a good essay reads like an invitation, and the people who accept it tend to stay. Our own numbers moved more from moving the form to the footer than from any change of wording we tried, and we tried a lot of wording.

Ask once, at the end, when the reader has had something worth staying for.

That sentence has been taped above my desk since the summer. It is not a rule so much as a test: if a prompt would still make sense to someone who has just finished reading, it can stay. If it only makes sense to someone who has just arrived, it is asking too early. The full set of notes, with the sites we looked at and what each one asked for, is on the website.

For paid subscribers, here is the part that is harder to say. Some of the popups we counted belong to publications we admire, and a few belong to us. The newsletter box on our own archive pages still opens after fifteen seconds, which is roughly the time it takes to read a first paragraph and decide whether to keep going. We are turning it off this week and watching what happens to signups from the archive. If they fall, we will say so here, with the numbers.

There is a version of this argument that ends with no asking at all, and I do not believe it. Readers who like a publication want to be told how to keep reading it. The trouble is only ever timing and volume: one clear question at the right moment, instead of three at the wrong one.

A better ask

  • Put the signup form after the post, where a reader who finished has a reason to want more.
  • Ask for one thing at a time: an email address, not an address, a name and a survey.
  • Say plainly what they will get and how often, so the yes is an informed one.

None of these ideas is new. What is new is how many small sites have taken on the habits of very large ones, where a popup is a rounding error on a big number. At our size every reader is a person we might keep for years, and the first thing they see should be the writing. Next week: what happened to the archive signups, and a short note on the welcome email, which is the other place we ask too early.
